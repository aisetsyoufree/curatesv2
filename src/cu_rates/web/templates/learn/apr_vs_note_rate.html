APR vs. Note Rate: Why They Are Never Interchangeable

Two Distinct Financial Measurements

When consumers review mortgage advertisements, lenders publish two interrelated yet distinct figures: the note interest rate and the Annual Percentage Rate (APR). Treating these two metrics as interchangeable is a significant error in financial evaluation.

Federal regulations, including the Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z, mandate the disclosure of both figures specifically because they communicate fundamentally different information regarding the cost of borrowing.

The Note Interest Rate

The note rate—also referred to as the nominal or contract interest rate—is the specific contractual percentage used by the loan servicer to compute the periodic interest charge:

  • It directly determines the monthly principal and interest payment due from the borrower.
  • It reflects only the cost of borrowing the principal balance itself and excludes any third-party fees, administrative charges, or upfront points.
  • On an adjustable-rate mortgage, the note rate adjusts over time based on the index and margin specified in the loan agreement.

The Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

The Annual Percentage Rate provides a broader calculation representing the total annualized cost of the credit transaction:

  • The APR incorporates the contract interest rate plus mandatory upfront finance charges, including discount points, origination charges, processing fees, and certain lender closing costs.
  • These prepaid finance charges are amortized over the full contractual life of the loan and expressed as an annualized percentage.
  • Because it reflects upfront financing charges alongside periodic interest, the APR is typically higher than the underlying note rate. If a loan carries zero closing fees and zero points, the note rate and APR converge.

Comparison Rules and Limitations

In public rate directories and loan comparisons, note rates must be compared exclusively with note rates, and APRs must be compared exclusively with APRs. A low note rate accompanied by high upfront fees will produce a significantly higher APR, whereas an offer with a slightly higher note rate but zero fees may yield a lower overall APR.

Furthermore, APR calculations assume the borrower retains the loan for its full contractual amortization period. If a loan is refinanced or paid off early, the effective annual cost will exceed the stated APR because the upfront closing fees are absorbed over fewer years.
