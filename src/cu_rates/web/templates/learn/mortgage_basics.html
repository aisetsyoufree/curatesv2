Mortgage Basics and Core Principles

What Is a Mortgage?

A mortgage is an agreement that gives the lender the right to take the property if the borrowed money, plus interest, is not repaid. People use a mortgage to buy a home or to borrow against a home they already own.

Core Components of a Mortgage

  • Principal: The amount borrowed. Part of each monthly payment reduces that balance.
  • Interest: What the lender charges for lending the money. The rest of the principal-and-interest payment goes to interest and does not reduce the balance.
  • Term: How long the borrower has to repay the loan.
  • Amortization: On a typical fixed-rate loan, the combined principal-and-interest payment stays the same, but the split changes. Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is still high. Later payments are mostly principal. That process is amortization.
  • Escrow: An account the lender sets up so part of the monthly payment can pay property-related expenses such as taxes and insurance. Many lenders require escrow. Sometimes the law requires it. Homeowner's insurance, property taxes, and private mortgage insurance are often added to the monthly payment.

Fixed-Rate vs. Adjustable-Rate Structures

  • Fixed-Rate Mortgages: The interest rate is set when the loan is made and does not change. On a typical fixed-rate loan, the principal-and-interest payment does not change over the life of the loan.
  • Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs): The rate may go up or down. Many ARMs start lower than a fixed-rate loan and keep that initial rate for months, one year, or a few years. After that, the rate changes on a set schedule. The rate is the index plus a set margin, subject to any caps on how much the rate can rise or fall.
